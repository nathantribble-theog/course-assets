# Marketing 101
Understanding customers, choosing your audience, and delivering value

Speaker notes: Welcome to Marketing 101. Most people hear "marketing" and think of ads, sales pitches, and promotions. Those are the most visible parts, but they're only the last step. At its core, marketing is about understanding what customers need, sometimes before they can put it into words, and then building a product, price, location, and message that meet that need better than anyone else. Today we'll build a framework you can use on any business: the 3Cs, STP, and the 4Ps. Then we'll look at why consumers buy, and finish with real campaigns that worked brilliantly and ones that backfired. Keep one question in mind throughout: who exactly is this for, and why would they care?

---

# More Than Just Selling

- **Marketing is more than just selling products.** Marketing is all the activities designed to satisfy customer needs through exchange.
- **Focus on understanding customer needs first.** The marketing concept means identifying needs first, then producing goods to satisfy them.
- **Use the "right" principle.** Get the right product to the right customers at the right time and place.

Takeaway: Marketing starts with understanding customer needs, then uses the "right" principle to deliver products that satisfy them.

Speaker notes: Selling is the final step in a long process, not the whole of marketing. The marketing concept flips the old "make it, then push it" mindset: first find out what customers need, including needs they haven't expressed yet, then build something that satisfies it. That's why companies like Apple and Amazon invest so much in research and observation. The "right" principle ties it together: the right product, for the right customer, at the right time and place. A great product at the wrong price or in the wrong channel still fails. Ask students: name a product you love. Which of the "rights" does that company nail? Which one would you change?

# The Ultimate Marketing Framework: 3C → STP → 4P

![The 3C STP 4P marketing framework](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nathantribble-theog/course-assets/main/marketing-101/images/03-3c-stp-4p-framework.jpg)

- **3C: Analyze.** Customer, Company, Competitor
- **STP: Choose.** Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning
- **4P: Execute.** Product, Price, Place, Promotion

Speaker notes: This is the roadmap for the rest of the lecture. The graphic comes from a Korean marketing textbook, which is a nice reminder that this framework is used all over the world. Read it left to right as analyze, choose, execute. The 3Cs are your situation analysis: who the customers are, what your company is good at, and what competitors offer. STP is the strategic choice: split the market into segments, pick the segments you can serve best, and decide how you want those customers to see you. The 4Ps are the tactics that bring the strategy to life. The order matters. Companies that jump straight to the 4Ps, such as running ads before they know who they're for, waste money. Every decision in the 4Ps should trace back to a choice made in STP.

---

# The 3Cs

- **Customer:** Gain deep insight into customer motivations and segments
- **Company:** Decide which customers to target and set marketing goals
- **Competitor:** Understand how customers see competitors so you can position effectively

Takeaway: The 3C framework focuses on understanding the Customer, the Company's goals, and the Competitor landscape.

Speaker notes: The 3Cs are where strategy begins. Customer: go beyond demographics to motivations, frustrations, and behaviors. Why do they buy, and what job are they hiring the product to do? Company: be honest about your strengths, resources, and goals. You can't serve everyone, so where do you have an edge? Competitor: what matters is how customers see your competitors, not how you see them. If customers think of a competitor as "the cheap one" or "the premium one," that shapes where you can stand. The magic happens where the three overlap: a customer need your company can meet better than competitors. Quick exercise: pick a local coffee shop and run its 3Cs out loud.

# STP: The Art of Finding Your Audience

- **STP stands for Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning.** It's a framework that helps businesses identify and reach their target audience.
- **Segmentation divides consumers into groups based on needs.** This lets firms match products to what each group wants.
- **Targeting focuses on the most viable customer segments.** Serving the target audience well builds loyalty and profitability.

Takeaway: STP is key to identifying and reaching the right target audience.

Speaker notes: Segmentation splits the market into groups with similar needs. You can segment by demographics (age, income), geography, psychographics (values, lifestyle), or behavior (usage rate, loyalty). Targeting means choosing which segments to serve, based on size, growth, profitability, and how well you can serve them. Positioning is the place you want to hold in the target customer's mind relative to competitors. Volvo means safety, Walmart means low prices, Porsche means performance heritage. Remember that last one, because we'll come back to Porsche at the end. The hardest part of STP is saying no to segments. Trying to appeal to everyone usually means you're memorable to no one.

# The Marketing Mix: The Four Ps

- **Product:** Physical goods, services, branding, packaging, warranties, and service programs
- **Price:** Pricing strategy based on demand, cost, and a fair return, including skimming and penetration pricing
- **Promotion:** Advertising, PR, social media, and e-commerce coordinated as one promotional mix
- **Place:** Distribution channels such as transportation, supply chain, inventory, and online presence

Speaker notes: The 4Ps turn strategy into action. Product is the whole offering, not just the physical item: brand, packaging, warranty, and the service experience. Price signals value. Skimming starts high and drops over time, like new phones and game consoles. Penetration starts low to grab market share quickly, like streaming services' first-month deals. Promotion is how you communicate: advertising, PR, social, influencers, and email, all coordinated so they tell one story. Place is how the product reaches the customer: stores, e-commerce, distribution partners, and logistics. The key word is mix. All four must be consistent with your positioning. A luxury product sold at discount prices in dollar stores sends a confused message.

# The 4Ps in Action: A Google Search for "Hotels in Los Angeles"

![Google search results for hotels in Los Angeles showing sponsored ads](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nathantribble-theog/course-assets/main/marketing-101/images/07-google-ads-hotels-la.jpg)

Speaker notes: Here's what happens when you search "hotels in Los Angeles." Look at how many of the 4Ps show up on one screen. Promotion: the sponsored listings at the top. Hotels bid to appear exactly when someone has shown intent to book, which is about as precise as targeting gets. Price: rates are shown right up front, along with "deal" tags and "18% less than usual" badges, so price competition is instant and transparent. Place: the map and booking links mean the channel and the ad are the same thing, because you can book without leaving the search. Product: star ratings, reviews, and photos show the whole offering. Google Ads charges per click, so advertisers pay only when someone shows interest, and every click is measurable. Ask students: which listing would you click, and why?

# Consumer Behavior: The Why Behind the Buy

- **Cultural, social, individual, and psychological factors influence consumer behavior.** Values, customs, traditions, social media, other people's opinions, gender, age, and personality all shape what people buy.
- **Consumer decision making involves multiple steps.** Need recognition, information search, evaluation, purchase, and post-purchase behavior.
- **Understanding consumer behavior helps marketers appeal to target consumers.** Marketers can then develop products, prices, channels, and messages that meet consumer needs and preferences.

Takeaway: Because consumer behavior is complex, understanding it is crucial for building marketing strategies that create value.

Speaker notes: Now we move from frameworks to the customer's head. Influences come in layers: culture and subculture, social groups and family, personal factors like age and lifestyle, and psychological factors like motivation, perception, and attitudes. The decision process has five steps. Need recognition: "I'm hungry." Information search: checking reviews and asking friends. Evaluation of alternatives. The purchase itself. Post-purchase behavior: satisfaction, regret, reviews, and repeat buying. Marketers can step in at every stage: create the need, show up in the search, win the comparison, make buying easy, and reassure people after the sale. Ask students to walk through the five steps for the last thing they bought over $50.

# The Importance of Customer Value and Satisfaction

[NEW AI IMAGE for this card: a warm, modern scene of a delighted customer at a shop counter receiving a purchase with a smile from a staff member, with subtle visual cues of loyalty (a returning-customer card, a heart, a five-star rating). Clean editorial style, no text in the image.]

Customer value and satisfaction are core marketing concepts. Companies that deliver superior value and satisfaction through relationship marketing earn customer loyalty and long-term business success.

- **Customer value** = perceived benefits ÷ perceived costs (money, time, effort), compared with the alternatives
- **Satisfaction** = actual performance ÷ expectations: below means disappointed, equal means satisfied, above means delighted
- **Loyalty:** delighted customers come back, buy more, and recommend you

Speaker notes: Customer value is the customer's perception of benefits received compared with what they gave up: money, time, and effort. It's always judged against the alternatives. Satisfaction is how actual performance compares with expectations. Meet expectations and customers are satisfied. Exceed them and they become delighted, and delighted customers come back and recommend you. That's why relationship marketing focuses on the long game: loyalty programs, personalized communication, great post-purchase support. Keeping a customer is far cheaper than winning a new one, and a loyal customer's lifetime value can be many times a single purchase. Example: Netflix wins on value through a huge library, personalization, and convenience at a competitive monthly price.

# Four Types of Consumer Decisions

[NEW DIAGRAM: build this as a native Gamma 2x2 matrix / four-quadrant layout with an icon in each quadrant. Horizontal axis: Functional (practical needs) → Emotional (feelings and identity). Vertical axis: Low involvement → High involvement.]

- **Utilitarian / Instrumental** (high involvement, functional): Does it do the job? *Examples: vacuum cleaner, TV, laptop.* Win with specs, comparisons, and reviews.
- **Low Involvement** (low involvement, functional): Routine and low-risk. *Examples: bottled water, ketchup, paper towels.* Win with availability, habit, and familiar packaging.
- **Hedonic** (low-to-moderate involvement, emotional): Driven by pleasure. *Examples: a concert, dessert, a weekend trip.* Win with sensory, experiential messaging.
- **Ego-Expressive** (high involvement, emotional): Says who I am. *Examples: designer suit, luxury handbag, a sports car.* Win with identity, status, and imagery.

Speaker notes: Not every purchase is made the same way. Utilitarian or instrumental decisions are about function, like a vacuum cleaner or a TV, where you compare specs, durability, and price. Low-involvement decisions are routine, low-risk buys like bottled water or ketchup, where convenience, availability, and habit win. Hedonic decisions are driven by pleasure and emotion: entertainment, travel, a great meal. Ego-expressive decisions say something about who you are: designer suits, luxury handbags, the car you drive. Strategy changes with each type. Utilitarian buyers want facts and comparisons, low-involvement buyers want easy availability and familiar packaging, and hedonic and ego-expressive buyers respond to imagery, identity, and status. Ask students to place three things they bought this week on the grid.

# How Consumers Process Marketing Messages

[NEW DIAGRAM: build this as a native Gamma horizontal process / arrow-steps layout with an icon for each step, then a final step showing the result.]

1. **Exposure:** The message physically reaches the consumer (ad, billboard, feed post)
2. **Attention:** It breaks through the clutter and gets noticed
3. **Perception:** The consumer interprets it through their own experiences and biases
4. **Comprehension:** The consumer understands, or misunderstands, the intended message
5. **Decision:** The message enters the buying process: need recognition → search → evaluation → purchase → post-purchase

Speaker notes: Before anyone buys, a marketing message has to get through four stages, and most messages fail along the way. Exposure: the message physically reaches the consumer through an ad, billboard, or feed post. But people skip, scroll, and mute. Attention: the message has to break through the clutter with striking visuals, surprise, or an unexpected place. Perception: people select, organize, and interpret what they see through their own experiences and biases, so two people can read the same ad completely differently. Comprehension: the consumer understands the intended message, or misunderstands it. Keep these four stages in mind for the next few slides. We'll look at a campaign that nailed all four, and one that badly misjudged perception.

# "Got Milk?" — Tom Brady, and Why the Campaign Worked

![Got Milk? ad featuring Tom Brady](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nathantribble-theog/course-assets/main/marketing-101/images/15-got-milk-tom-brady.jpg)

- **Reversed declining sales:** California milk sales rose about 7% in the first year
- **Deprivation strategy:** Highlighted the pain of running out of milk instead of listing nutrition facts
- **Cultural impact:** The tagline and milk mustache became pop-culture icons
- **Broad reach:** TV, print, and outdoor ads, with celebrities matched to each segment

Speaker notes: Tom Brady likes his milk with ice. Maybe you can be the GOAT too? Now step back and ask why this campaign is considered one of the most successful ever. Milk sales were falling as soft drinks took over. The original "Got Milk?" TV spots used a deprivation strategy: instead of saying milk is healthy, they showed the frustration of having a cookie or cereal and no milk. California milk sales rose about 7% in the first year. The celebrity milk-mustache ads then extended the idea to segment after segment: athletes for sports fans, pop stars for teens, supermodels for the fashion-conscious. It's a strong example of a simple, consistent message tailored to each target segment.

# When Marketing Backfires: Reebok, Germany 2012

![Reebok ad: Cheat on your girlfriend, not on your workout](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nathantribble-theog/course-assets/main/marketing-101/images/16-reebok-cheat-ad.jpg)

Speaker notes: In March 2012, Reebok put up this ad in partner gyms in Germany: "Cheat on your girlfriend, not on your workout." It went viral for all the wrong reasons. Thousands of complaints poured in, and Reebok took it down. Connect this to the four stages: the ad nailed exposure and attention, but it badly misjudged perception. The creative team saw an edgy joke about commitment to fitness. Much of the audience saw it as endorsing infidelity and alienating women, a huge share of Reebok's customers. Lesson: when an ad goes viral, you lose control of who sees it. Always ask how your message reads to people outside your intended audience. Discussion: was this a targeting failure, a positioning failure, or both?

# Place as Promotion: Bonux Bus-Top Ads in Lebanon

![Bonux detergent ads painted on bus roofs, seen from above](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nathantribble-theog/course-assets/main/marketing-101/images/17-bonux-bus-top-ads.jpg)

Speaker notes: Here's a brilliant example of choosing the medium as carefully as the message. Bonux, a laundry detergent, painted its ads on bus roofs in Lebanon. Why the roof? Think about the target customer: the person doing the household laundry, often hanging clothes to dry on apartment balconies above busy streets. From the balcony, they look down, and there's Bonux, at the exact moment they're thinking about laundry. This is the 4Ps working together. Promotion: a moving billboard. Place: dense urban streets where the target customers live. Product: reinforced in the context where it's used. It's also cost-effective compared with traditional billboards. Ask students: where's an unexpected place your target customer looks every day?

# Nissan vs. Porsche: Bigger Isn't Better

| | Nissan | Porsche |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 annual revenue | ~$77.5B | ~$40B |
| 2023 profit | ~$1.71B | ~$4.2B |
| Profit per $1 of revenue | ~2¢ | ~10¢ |

Porsche earns about half of Nissan's revenue but more than twice its profit.

Speaker notes: Here's why positioning matters to the bottom line. Nissan brings in nearly twice Porsche's revenue, yet Porsche earns well over twice the profit. Nissan keeps roughly 2 cents of every dollar of sales. Porsche keeps about 10. That's the power of a strong brand and a well-chosen target segment: Porsche customers pay a premium for performance and identity. Remember ego-expressive purchases from earlier. Nissan competes in crowded, price-sensitive segments where margins are thin. Note: these figures are rounded approximations for discussion. Exchange rates and profit definitions vary by source, but the gap in margins is the point. Ask students: would you rather run the bigger company or the more profitable one?

Speaker notes: These classic print ads show Porsche's positioning in action. "Nobody's perfect" celebrates the 1983 Le Mans 24 Hours, where Porsches took nine of the top ten places. The only non-Porsche finished ninth. It's a brag dressed up as humility. "In Germany there are no getaway cars" plays on the fact that even the police drive Porsches. All four speak to a narrow, passionate segment that values performance, heritage, and standing out. That's STP done right.

# Key Takeaways

1. **Start with the customer.** Marketing begins with understanding needs, not with selling.
2. **Analyze, choose, execute.** Use 3C → STP → 4P, in that order.
3. **Know why people buy.** Decision type and the path from exposure to comprehension shape your message.
4. **Mind your perception.** A message that grabs attention can still backfire (Reebok).
5. **Positioning pays.** Porsche shows that a focused brand can beat a bigger rival on profit.

Speaker notes: Let's recap. Marketing starts with understanding customer needs. The 3C, STP, and 4P framework turns that understanding into strategy and action, and the order matters. Consumer behavior explains why people buy, and the type of decision tells you which message will work. Getting attention isn't enough, because perception decides whether a message lands or backfires, as Reebok learned. Finally, strong positioning creates real financial value: Porsche turns a focused brand into some of the highest margins in the car industry. For next time, pick a brand you love and map it through the 3Cs, STP, and 4Ps.
